Failure mode #1

Goodhart's Law.

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
— Charles Goodhart, 1975

What it means here

XP is computed from logged hours. The moment XP starts mattering for evaluation, engineers start optimizing for hours-logged, not work-done. The hours stop being a useful signal of effort — they become a number to maximize.

Why it's hard to dodge

It's not a question of whether engineers are honest. It's that any rational person, told the score affects their career, will tilt their behavior toward the score. Every studied incentive system has shown this drift — sales quotas, school test scores, hospital wait times, police arrest counts. None are exceptions.

The dashboard's first promise — "honest, continuous visibility into growth" — is in direct tension with its second promise — "the KPIs you'll be graded on." The two cannot both stay true once the system has teeth.

Case study: the electronic whip

Disneyland put a productivity scoreboard on the wall.

In Disneyland's industrial laundry, management installed a real-time scoreboard showing each worker's output. They expected a productivity boost. They got one — for a while — alongside several things they didn't expect.

Injuries went up

Workers sped up to keep their numbers visible. The injury rate climbed.

Bathroom breaks got skipped

Stepping away meant your number froze. People held it.

Pregnant workers fell behind — visibly

The board didn't care about context. Their score told a story they had no way to correct.

Coworkers turned on each other

The team was previously collegial. The board converted teammates into competitors. Slow workers were resented, not helped.

Workers nicknamed the scoreboard "the electronic whip." That phrase — coined by the people the system was supposedly helping — is the part to remember.

Case study: the leaderboard backfire

Omnicare's helpdesk gamification made everything worse.

Omnicare, a pharmacy software company, gamified customer service: a public leaderboard ranking response speed, with cash bonuses for the fastest responders. Reasonable on paper.

Wait times went up

Staff cherry-picked easy tickets to inflate their counts. Hard tickets sat.

Turnover went up

Employees described it as "Big Brother." Good people quit faster than bad ones could be replaced.

Trust collapsed

A measurement system without explicit consent reads as surveillance, even when it's framed as motivation.

The intent was identical to ours: make the work visible, reward the right behaviors, motivate growth. The execution looked reasonable. The outcome was every metric the system was meant to improve, going the wrong way.

The closest existing system

Billable hours: 50 years of data on what hours-as-target does to people.

Law firms, consulting firms, and accounting firms have run an enormous, decades-long experiment on tying hours-logged to evaluation. The results are not subtle.

Measurable burnout and depression

Lawyers with billable targets above 1800 hours/year show significantly higher rates of burnout, depressive symptoms, and psychological distress than peers without targets.

Padding is universal

Time-padding is so widespread it's an open secret. The American Bar Association has published ethics guidelines specifically on it. The system rewards inefficiency: charging a customer per hour incentivizes dragging the work out.

Incivility and bullying go up

As targets rose, peer-reported experiences of incivility, bullying, and discriminatory behavior rose with them. Hours-pressure corrodes the team's social fabric.

Quality drops

A senior who solves a problem in 30 minutes earns less than a junior who grinds for a day. The system inverts the actual value gradient and over time, top performers either leave or learn to drag their feet.

Hours-as-target is one of the best-studied bad incentive systems in the modern economy. Any new system built on the same primitive inherits the same risks unless it explicitly designs them out.

Failure mode #2

Engineers experience badges as condescending.

Sales floors, call centers, and support teams accept gamified dashboards. Engineering teams, on average, do not. The reasons are cultural and they are durable.

XP for inputs feels infantilizing

Senior engineers don't want a "Deep Focus" badge for spending a week on one project. They want their judgment trusted. Badges signal "we're tracking your inputs because we don't trust your outputs."

Hours don't measure value — especially for seniors

A senior who unblocks three teammates in 30 minutes produces more value than a junior who logs 40 hours of grinding. An hours-based XP system rewards the wrong person and everyone on the team can see it.

The stack-ranking memory

A generation of engineers remembers Microsoft's stack ranking and Amazon's URA. Anything that scores them on a continuous numeric axis triggers that pattern-match, fairly or not.

Hardware doesn't fit software gamification patterns

Most gamification tools target sales (calls/closes) or developers (commits/PRs). Mechanical and electronics work has neither. Forcing hours as the proxy can feel arbitrary to the people doing the work.

Specific risks for this team

Five ways this dashboard could go wrong here.

1

Comp coupling

The day a leader says "your bonus is based on XP," the dashboard becomes the billable hour. Every problem documented in this deck arrives at once. This is the single biggest risk.

2

Padding

Engineers begin logging 10-hour days for 7 hours of work. The data corrupts itself, silently. Manager loses the very visibility the system was built to provide.

3

Public shaming via radar

An engineer with a narrow skill profile has it visible to peers. Strengths read as gaps. The "celebrate growth" frame collapses the moment someone feels the chart embarrasses them.

4

Level-as-time-served

If leveling depends only on accumulated hours-in-bucket, anyone can level up by clocking time in the right project — without ever demonstrating the skill. The level number becomes meaningless and engineers stop trusting it.

5

"Why does Pluem only have 6,200 XP this quarter?"

A leader sees the number and reacts to it. The number is now load-bearing in conversations it was never meant to support. The dashboard was built as a mirror — it has just become a dial.
